# Neighborhood Kitchen Business Plan

Version 1.5  |  Product planning draft  |  10 October 2026

### Making room for good food

Building a livelihood should leave room for eating well. Yet busy schedules can make everyday home cooking difficult.

Neighborhood Kitchen connects households seeking home-cooked meals with eligible neighbors who enjoy preparing them. Households gain time and visibility into their food choices; cooks gain an opportunity to earn additional income from their passion, offering meals they also prepare for their own families.

“We Sell What We Eat” expresses our commitment to care and transparency—helping neighbors keep good food a priority alongside work and family.

### Vision and mission

Our vision is to make everyday home-cooked meals accessible within neighborhoods. Our mission is to make it simple for ordinary residents to find meals prepared by nearby home cooks, understand what is in them, and coordinate pickup with confidence.

The community motto is “We Sell What We Eat.” Cooks affirm that they prepare the offered food with the same care and ingredients they use for their own families. This is a community promise; it does not establish nutritional quality or replace food-safety requirements.

### The product promise

Open the app and answer “What is cooking in my neighborhood?” Explore published offers with cook-set prices, ingredients, sourcing, allergens, planned preparation dates, pickup windows and a required “Contact/order by” deadline. Contact the cook outside the app to confirm availability and arrange ordering, payment and pickup. One account supports both discovering meals and becoming an eligible cook.

### Advance publishing and expiration

Cooks publish the meals they intend to offer, including a week ahead, and choose a contact/order deadline no later than pickup start. For example, neighbors contact the cook by Tuesday at 6 p.m. for Thursday pickup from 5–7 p.m. At Tuesday’s deadline the offer stops appearing in discovery and assistant results. Existing links show “Ordering deadline has passed” and disable the ordering contact action. Cooks can edit or close an offer early; deadline extensions require an explicit action. The app does not know whether any external order was placed. Advance publication retains the cook-reported same-day preparation and never-freeze-completed-meal commitment.

Proposals in this plan—pilot size, targets, staffing, technology, and policies—are working defaults for validation, not additional founder decisions.

## Customer problem and initial market

### The problem to validate

Busy households may want home-cooked food but lack time to prepare it consistently. Nearby residents may already be cooking meals and be willing to offer additional portions. Today those connections can depend on personal contacts and fragmented group messages. The opportunity is to make availability and trust information easier to discover without asking neighbors to operate a restaurant-style storefront.

Home-cooked food is not automatically healthier, and restaurant meals are not uniformly unhealthy. The product should support informed choices through concrete disclosures rather than advertise unsupported health benefits. Nutrition calculations and medical dietary advice are outside the initial scope.

| Participant | Need | What the pilot tests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Busy household | An available meal that fits preferences and pickup timing. | Can they find a suitable offer and understand how to contact the cook in two minutes? |
| Occasional home cook | An easy way to offer portions on chosen days. | Is publishing manageable and is demand predictable enough? |
| Neighbor who does both | Buy on some days and cook on others. | Can one account support both activities naturally? |
| Household with dietary constraints | Clear ingredients and allergen information. | Can disclosures support a choice without implying an allergen-free kitchen? |

### Initial geographic strategy

Start in one compact neighborhood within a jurisdiction that supports the intended home-meal activity. The Bay Area is a reasonable planning context given the founder’s location, but no city or county is selected. Confirm local authorization, platform classification, and cook eligibility before opening real listings. Geographic density matters more than total registrations.

### Market sizing method

Do not invent a national market size. For the selected pilot area, count households reachable through approved community channels, interview likely buyers and cooks, and measure actual recurring supply. Estimate demand from household interviews and voluntary research surveys. Compare it with recurring published offers, pickup overlap and dietary fit. Contact clicks measure interest, not orders or sales; the MVP has no transaction or inventory data.

### Research before a live pilot

- Interview 10–15 households and 5–10 prospective home cooks; these are proposed research sample sizes.

- Ask about recent meal decisions, pickup tolerance, desired disclosures, and participation barriers rather than hypothetical enthusiasm alone.

- Test prototype tasks with sample food and profiles. Observe completion time, trust questions, and drop-off.

- Record which concerns require product changes and which require operational assistance or local clarification.

### Differentiating combination

The proposed differentiation is a resident-only network where the same person can discover and offer meals, listings describe food the cook also prepares for their household, and ingredient sourcing is structured. Approximate public location preserves neighborhood discovery without exposing home addresses. Cooks share exact pickup details directly with neighbors outside the platform.

These are positioning choices, not proven unique capabilities. Competitors can copy features. A durable advantage would need to emerge from neighborhood density, repeat relationships, useful offers and trusted communication, and practical cook onboarding. Ingredient disclosure alone is not a defensible moat.

Neighborhood Kitchen connects neighbors through meals prepared on the day of pickup and never frozen after preparation. Cooks confirm this commitment when publishing, with the same care they bring to their own family meals. Simple in-app disclosures and scheduled pickup support trust without unnecessary work for cooks. The declaration is cook-reported, not independent verification. The app shows the planned preparation date and whether pickup is hot or chilled; this policy does not require printed labels. Applicable local labeling requirements still apply.

### Why someone would choose it

The hypothesis is that neighbors value familiar everyday food, transparent preparation details, and convenient nearby pickup enough to use the network repeatedly. The pilot must test that against existing habits. Excluding commercial operators may protect the community identity while reducing supply and hours of availability.

## AI assistance in the MVP

### One assistant with two entry points

The MVP includes “Help me find a meal” for consumers and “Help me offer a meal” for cooks within the same account. Consumers describe household preferences in everyday language, review interpreted preferences and explore available matches. Cooks describe a meal, receive a draft and questions about missing information, then review and confirm the facts before publication.

### Product benefit

The assistant aims to reduce the effort of searching and entering listings while preserving ingredient transparency and cook accountability. It can explain meal matches and answer questions from approved user-facing guidance. It does not invent ingredients, sourcing, nutritional values or safety assurances, and it does not replace eligibility review or the same-day preparation commitment.

### Grounded information

Cook-published records supply stated prices, ingredients, planned pickup details and contact/order deadlines. These are offers, not live inventory: consumers must confirm availability directly with the cook. The app excludes expired, withdrawn and ineligible offers from search and assistant recommendations using the current server time. Document-based retrieval supplies approved platform guidance with sources. LangChain coordinates a bounded workflow; one model integration can serve both entry points. Business plans and PRDs guide developers and are not automatically consumer-policy content.

### Prototype and validation

Initial wireframes simulate assistant interactions. The subsequent functional prototype uses a real model and real retrieval over fictional meals and clearly labeled sample guidance, while food activity remains simulated. Test whether consumers find suitable meals faster and cooks complete accurate listings with less effort; measure unsupported claims, preference mistakes, response time and cost alongside task success.

### Scope and sustainability

The MVP covers discovery, meal questions, listing drafts and platform help. Contacting a cook outside the system and publishing a reviewed offer remain explicit user actions. Autonomous ordering, medical or nutritional advice, automatic compliance approval and unrelated general-purpose chat are excluded. Budget for model calls, embeddings, retrieval maintenance and evaluation; retain conventional filters and forms when AI is unavailable.

## Operating model and trust

### Participation policy

Eligible cooks are residents preparing meals in their own homes under the applicable rules. A neighbor who obtains a required home-kitchen permit may legally be a business and remains eligible under this policy. Exclusion is based on established commercial operations and preparation model, not simply the presence of a business registration or permit.

Onboarding includes a residential preparation declaration, neighborhood eligibility, relevant permit or credential review, and consent to required disclosures. Public badges must describe exactly what was checked and when. Do not use “safe,” “healthy,” or “fully verified” as blanket assurances.

### Required transparency

- List ingredients and relevant compound ingredients, allergens, and possible kitchen cross-contact.

- Describe source by ingredient where known. “Not known” is visible and honest; receipts are not required for every ingredient.

- State organic status per ingredient as cook-reported, not organic, or unknown. Do not imply certification without evidence.

- Explain portion size, preparation day, pickup window, and food-specific storage or reheating instructions where appropriate.

- Show the required permit information and jurisdiction disclosures without exposing private verification documents.

### Operational responsibilities

| Owner role | Responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| Founder and product | Mission, participant policy, pilot boundary, research, scope decisions. |
| Pilot operations lead | Cook reviews, permit renewal checks, offer and contact-route support, incident escalation. |
| Engineering owner | Access controls, deadline enforcement, offer integrity, backups and audit records. |
| Local regulatory adviser | Confirm applicable platform and cook obligations, disclosures, retention, and reporting. |
| Home cook | Accurate offers and deadlines, lawful preparation, direct ordering, payment and pickup arrangements; close offers early when needed. |

### Incident handling

Reports remain available from offers and cook profiles. Operators triage suspected illness, allergen misrepresentation, commercial misuse and harassment. Serious safety concerns can trigger immediate listing suspension and direct outreach where participants are known. The platform cannot identify everyone who ordered outside the app. Preserve offer revisions and report evidence with restricted access, and follow applicable reporting rules; no order records are collected. Support coverage and response targets must be staffed before the pilot opens.

## Launch strategy and sustainability

### Prototype and live pilot are different releases

The prototype uses fictional cooks, meals, addresses, and credentials, clearly labeled as sample data. Contact actions are simulated and cannot contact real cooks, arrange real payment or create live food offers. It can validate the experience without making a claim that regulatory requirements disappear.

A live pilot enables real food listings only after the chosen jurisdiction and participation model are reviewed. California guidance identifies home-kitchen permitting and platform registration obligations [4–6]. A no-commission model or payment outside the app is not sufficient evidence of exemption. The precise classification of a non-revenue platform needs current local and legal confirmation.

### Proposed pilot sequence

| Stage | Scope | Evidence to proceed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Discovery | Interviews and sample-data prototype. | Core tasks understood; material trust questions identified. |
| Readiness | Select jurisdiction and review model; recruit eligible home cooks. | Written requirements, approved disclosures, verified cooks, staffed incident handling. |
| Closed pilot | Proposed 5–10 cooks and 25–50 households over 4–6 weeks. | Observed repeat use, manageable cook effort, useful discovery and clear direct contact. |
| Expansion decision | Review results before adding geography or delivery. | Demand and recurring supply justify cost and local requirements are resolved. |

### Costs even without monetization

Budget for hosting, database and media storage, email or SMS, verification, legal review, support, insurance decisions, and incident handling. No vendor quotes or spending authority have been established. Before a live pilot, record one-time setup costs, monthly costs, an agreed founder-funded spending ceiling, and the support hours available each week.

Track monthly platform cost per active participant and operator minutes per reviewed cook or report. Do not calculate fulfillment rates or sales from contact clicks. Cook economics are separate: providers choose prices and bear ingredient, preparation, packaging, and applicable compliance costs. Interviews should check whether offering extra portions remains worthwhile without the platform prescribing prices.

### Future sustainability options

Keep the MVP neutral about monetization. If recurring use develops, evaluate optional subscriptions, community sponsorship, or transaction fees with participant research and updated regulatory review. Do not insert advertising, paid ranking, or commissions into the initial experience. Any later funding model must preserve transparent fees and the neighborhood participation policy.

## Measures decisions and evidence

| Measure | Definition | Proposed pilot target |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Discovery speed | Time from browsing to finding a suitable meal in usability sessions. | Median under 2 minutes. |
| Publishing effort | Time to publish after cook approval; measure first and repeat listings separately. | Median repeat listing under 5 minutes. |
| Contact intent | Unique authenticated consumers opening an external contact action for an active offer. | Establish a baseline; not an order or conversion measure. |
| Deadline correctness | Expired offers excluded from browsing, search and assistant results, including cached pages. | 100 percent in deterministic boundary tests. |
| Repeat discovery | Households returning to browse or contact a cook within 30 days of first discovery use. | At least 30 percent; proposed learning target. |
| Disclosure completeness | Published meals passing required-field checks. | 100 percent. |
| Recurring supply | Approved cooks who publish again within 30 days of first publication. | At least 60 percent. |

Targets are proposed learning thresholds, not industry benchmarks or forecasts. Report denominators and small sample sizes. Also track complaints, unmatched searches, early offer closures, cook dropout reasons and operating effort. Use voluntary interviews to learn about off-platform experiences; the app cannot observe order acceptance, payments or completion. Zero reported incidents does not prove safety.

### Decisions still required

- Select the pilot neighborhood and confirm the applicable legal model.

- Approve the verification method, participant policies, address disclosure rule, and support coverage.

- Approve a pilot budget and insurance approach; disclose actual coverage accurately.

- Validate advance publishing, deadline comprehension and external contact preferences with households and cooks.

- Choose deployment and authentication providers after reviewing their current capabilities and costs.

### Recommended companion work

The PRD includes a development backlog, acceptance tests, and release gates. Before live participation, create a short operating playbook for cook onboarding and incidents, the applicable community and privacy policies, and a pilot research scorecard. These support the two primary documents; they do not require expanding the initial product.
